# 3. The peso circuit

The Treasury and the Banco Central de la República Argentina (BCRA, the central bank) run the peso circuit. The BCRA creates pesos when it buys foreign currency, transfers profits to the Treasury or pays interest, and absorbs them when it sells foreign currency or places interest-bearing liabilities. The Treasury collects taxes, spends, places peso debt and rolls it over. This chapter goes through both balance sheets and shows the most important change of the term that began in December 2023: the absorption of pesos, and its cost, moved from the BCRA to the Treasury.

Figures are in trillion pesos at August 2026 prices, deflated month by month with the consumer price index (CPI) of the Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Censos (INDEC, the national statistics office, SRC-2028), unless "current pesos" is stated. One peso of December 2023 is equivalent to 3.47 pesos at August 2026 prices.

::: {.destacado custom-style="Destacado"}
**The cost of absorbing pesos moved from the BCRA to the Treasury, and a large part of it accumulates outside the published fiscal balance.**

Until July 2024 the BCRA paid ARS 34.6 trillion at August 2026 prices in interest on the pesos it absorbed. Since then the Treasury pays, mostly as interest added to the principal of its bills and bonds: between 5 and 11 trillion current pesos per quarter, about 66 trillion up to June 2026. The cash-basis balance does not record it until it is paid, and then records it as amortization.

*Scope.* Capitalized interest increases the Treasury's obligations even when it is not paid in the period. The series does not include the principal adjustment of CER bonds or the interest on reopenings of TAMAR and dual bonds.
:::

## 3.1 From the BCRA to the Treasury: the transfer of interest-bearing liabilities

At the end of 2023, the BCRA owed ARS 25.8 trillion in current pesos in pases (BCRA repos absorbing pesos) and Letras de Liquidez (LELIQ, BCRA liquidity bills), 2.68 times the monetary base. That debt paid the monetary policy rate, which from 18 December was 100% in nominal annual terms. Because the pases were overnight and each day's interest was placed again the next day, the compounded yield over a year reached 171%: (1 + 1/365)^365 − 1 = 1.71. This is the effective rate the BCRA itself publishes. Until 15 December the rate had been 133% nominal on 28-day LELIQ, which compounded monthly gave a 255% effective rate, and part of the year-end stock still earned that rate (BCRA, Series.xlsm, SRC-1625). In its June 2026 monetary policy report, the BCRA itself notes that its securities and pases reached 2.5 times the base towards the end of 2023.

In two and a half years that stock disappeared from the BCRA's balance sheet:

| End of month | Monetary base | Pases and LELIQ | LEFI held by banks | Total interest-bearing | Interest-bearing / base |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Dec 2023 | 9.6 | 25.8 | | 25.8 | 2.68 |
| Jun 2024 | 20.2 | 15.9 | | 15.9 | 0.79 |
| Jul 2024 | 21.8 | 0 | 9.6 | 9.6 | 0.44 |
| Dec 2024 | 29.7 | 0 | 10.7 | 10.7 | 0.36 |
| Jun 2025 | 37.2 | 0 | 11.0 | 11.0 | 0.30 |
| Dec 2025 | 43.0 | | | 0.8 | 0.02 |
| Jul 2026 | 46.2 | | | 1.6 | 0.04 |

*Trillion current pesos. Source: BCRA, Series.xlsm, BCRA instruments sheet (SRC-1625). From July 2025 the interest-bearing stock consists of repos with banks.*

The transfer had three stages.

1. **LELIQ became pases** in December 2023, and pases declined during the first half of 2024 as banks exchanged them for Treasury LECAP.
2. **Pases became LEFI** in July 2024. The Letras Fiscales de Liquidez were Treasury bills (RC 40/2024), placed through an exchange with the BCRA, traded only between the BCRA and banks, and paid the policy rate with daily capitalization. They reached ARS 16.6 trillion held by banks in November 2024. The BCRA described the operation as the "traspaso de todos los pasivos remunerados al Tesoro" (transfer of all interest-bearing liabilities to the Treasury).
3. **LEFI were redeemed** on 10 July 2025 and Treasury LECAP, BONCAP and duals, placed at auction, took their place.

As a result, the interest the BCRA used to pay moved to the Treasury. LEFI accrued about ARS 3.4 trillion in current pesos of interest, collected by banks. The rest of the cost of absorbing pesos remained in the Treasury's peso debt, most of which capitalizes interest (section 3.4).

Several readings follow from the diagram.

**The BCRA paid interest until July 2024 and almost nothing afterwards.** The interest factor adds up to 34.6 trillion: 8.3 in December 2023 and 26.4 between January and July 2024. From August 2024 it is close to zero. The BCRA's financial statements show the same profile: interest losses of 28.0 trillion current pesos in 2023, 17.1 in 2024 and 1.7 in 2025.

**Through purchases of foreign currency, the Treasury returned to the BCRA almost everything it received in profits.** Transferred profits add up to 42.9 trillion (11.98 trillion current pesos in 2025, for the 2024 financial year, and 24.4 in May 2026, for the 2025 financial year). Over the same period, the Treasury bought foreign currency from the BCRA for 43.5 trillion. In May 2026 the profit transfer returned almost entirely to the BCRA in the same month: 24.4 trillion went out as profits and 22.8 came back as other operations with the Treasury, in line with the buyback of Letras Intransferibles (non-transferable Treasury notes held by the BCRA) for ARS 18.4 trillion (chapter 5).

**The profits are mostly accounting profits.** The BCRA Charter (Carta Orgánica, article 38, SRC-0012) requires the transfer to the Treasury of the year's profits that are neither capitalized nor allocated to reserves, and what it measures is the accounting result, not the cash collected. In 2024 the BCRA earned 19.4 trillion pesos of December 2024, but valuation items and accrued uncollected interest added up to 43.6: the CER adjustment on the Treasury bonds in its portfolio (27.2), the revaluation of its dollar assets due to the rise in the exchange rate (8.7) and the accrual on Letras Intransferibles and on advances to the Treasury (7.6). Without them, the BCRA would have lost about 17 trillion, mostly because of interest on pases. In 2025 it earned 34.3 trillion pesos of December 2025, and the same items added up to 32.0, 93% of the result. The largest cash gain that year was 6.3 trillion, from the buyback of Letras Intransferibles that the Treasury paid with IMF dollars (financial statements 2024 and 2025, SRC-1157 and SRC-1156, note 4.22; the split between cash and accounting items is our own).

A large part of that accounting profit is a claim on the Treasury itself that gets revalued: the CER on its bonds and the exchange rate on its Letras. Consolidating the public sector, the BCRA's gain is an equal loss for the Treasury. Even so, the profit transfer gives the Treasury pesos that can be spent, and during the term the Treasury largely used them to buy dollars from the BCRA itself.

**The Treasury does not cover losses.** The same article 38 states that a year's losses are charged to reserves and, if these are not enough, to the BCRA's capital, and that the board can use the following years' profits to rebuild them. That was done with the 2023 loss: it was charged to reserves and capital in 2024, and in 2025 the BCRA retained 7.7 trillion of the 2024 profits to rebuild them before transferring the rest. The Charter does not oblige the Treasury to recapitalize the BCRA when it makes a loss.

**Purchases of foreign currency from the private sector were the main source of pesos.** They added up to 64.7 trillion, concentrated in the first half of 2024 and in 2026. In 2025, under the exchange-rate band, the BCRA bought almost nothing.

**The monetary base grew by 63.5 trillion.** This is what remained in the economy after all operations.

A note on the source: an intermediate version of the factors table was wrong in 27 of the 45 months, because it mixed the annual total with the month of January. The code reads the BCRA's original spreadsheet and checks that the factors add up to the change in the base in each month (chapter 7).

**The balance.** The primary surplus for the period was 46.1 trillion. Cash interest, in all currencies, was 44.6, so the overall balance on a cash basis came to +1.5 trillion. In current pesos, the primary balance went from −2.0 trillion in December 2023 to +10.4 in 2024, +11.8 in 2025 and +12.3 between January and August 2026.

**Cash interest is almost all in dollars.** Of the 44.6 trillion of interest paid in cash, only 2.2 corresponds to peso debt. The rest, about 42.4 trillion, is interest on foreign-currency debt (table A.5 of the Finance Secretariat bulletin, by currency of payment, converted at each month's exchange rate). Peso debt pays almost no cash interest because it capitalizes: LECAP, BONCAP and LEFI accumulate interest in the principal and pay it at maturity, when the payment is recorded as amortization. The official financing program states that "los intereses se pagan con superávit fiscal" (interest is paid with the fiscal surplus) (SRC-1770). In practice, the primary surplus in pesos was enough to pay interest that was mostly in dollars, and that therefore required obtaining dollars.

**What financial assets are.** In the Budget Secretariat (Secretaría de Hacienda) table, the "increase in financial assets" is everything the public sector does with pesos other than spending or paying debt: depositing them at the BCRA or at Banco Nación, buying securities (the Fondo de Garantía de Sustentabilidad of ANSES, the pension system's reserve fund, or FGS, buys Treasury bonds), lending to provinces or companies. The "use of financial assets" is the opposite: withdrawing deposits, selling securities, collecting loans. Both lines are gross and move a lot from month to month. In net terms, during the term the public sector accumulated 75.6 trillion in financial assets, mostly deposits at the BCRA.

**Peso debt grew.** 610.5 trillion was placed and 507.8 amortized: net placement of 102.8 trillion. After deducting the 75.6 trillion the public sector accumulated in assets, net peso financing was about 27 trillion. Part of the debt placed above maturities stayed on deposit, as the BCRA said in April 2026. Those 27 trillion are debt, net of deposits, with parties outside the national public sector: banks, funds, individuals, but also the BCRA and Banco Nación, which are not part of it. Cash-basis sources do not allow separating how much is with private holders.

**The crossing into dollars.** The Treasury applied 72.7 trillion to dollar obligations: 45.7 to buying dollars and 27.0 to canceling dollar debt to the BCRA, which gave it no available dollars (chapter 5). Separately, it paid 79.3 trillion in pesos on dollar-denominated debt and received 31.9 from placing it. The peso sources for the period were the primary surplus (46.1), BCRA profits (42.9) and net placement of peso debt (102.8), while deposits were accumulating. The published sources do not show which peso financed what. There is no purchase-by-purchase release stating which pesos were used to buy the dollars.

## 3.4 The interest that goes unseen

LECAP and BONCAP capitalize interest and pay it all at maturity. On a cash basis, that interest does not appear as spending while it accumulates, and at maturity it is paid as amortization of principal. The published overall balance never records it. The Chief of Cabinet's Office (Jefatura de Gabinete) confirmed this in Report 144 (question 58): capitalized interest is not recorded as interest on a cash basis.

It helps to name three different measures of the balance precisely:

- the **overall balance on a cash basis** is the one the Secretaría de Hacienda publishes: it records interest when it is paid in cash;
- the **overall balance with accrued capitalized interest** adds the interest that LECAP, BONCAP and LEFI accumulate in principal each quarter, even if not yet paid. This chapter reconstructs it;
- an **inflation-adjusted balance** would also treat the principal adjustment of CER bonds, and the part of nominal interest that only compensates for inflation, consistently across all peso debt. The report does not calculate it.

The report reconstructs the second measure quarter by quarter with the Finance Secretariat's quarterly spreadsheets, net of what reopenings, discounts and placement premiums bring in.

Net capitalized interest ranges from 5 to 11 trillion current pesos per quarter since mid-2024 and adds up to about 66 trillion current pesos up to June 2026. With accrued capitalized interest, the overall balance of the national public sector is in deficit in every quarter since the third quarter of 2024. That interest is, to a large extent, the cost of absorbing pesos that the BCRA stopped paying in July 2024: it changed payer and recording method.

The measure has a known omission, which works against the balance. It leaves out the inflation adjustment of the principal of CER bonds, which the spreadsheet records separately and which ranges from 7 to 41 trillion current pesos per quarter, and the interest on reopenings of TAMAR and dual bonds and on exchanges. If the CER adjustment were added, the accrued overall balance in nominal terms would fall below the red bar. In an inflation-adjusted measure, by contrast, that adjustment and the inflationary part of interest on all peso debt would be treated as compensation for the loss of value of principal, and the deficit would be smaller than the nominal one. The two readings answer different questions, and the report presents the red bar under its own name: the nominal balance with capitalized interest, without the CER adjustment.

## 3.5 Fiscal scale: universities and interest

In the fiscal debate of the term, funding for national universities took a central place. It is useful to see its weight in the accounts next to other budget flows, with the same source, the same period and the same unit.

|---|---:|
| Transfers to national universities | 6.0 |
| Interest on debt, according to the budget | 13.9 |
| Current transfers in energy and transport | 9.9 |
| Social security benefits | 77.1 |
| *Outside the budget: net capitalized interest on peso debt (section 3.4)* | *39.6 to 46.9* |

*Sources: Presupuesto Abierto (open budget portal), 2025 annual appropriation, accrued (Secretaría de Hacienda, DGSIAF repository, updated to 5 July 2026); universities: program 26 "Desarrollo de la Educación Superior" (Development of Higher Education), item 5, sub-item 6; interest: item 7, interest only, excluding fees and premiums; energy and transport: current transfers under those functions, our own approximation to economic subsidies. Capitalized interest: own calculations, 2025 quarters, between the national public sector excluding the FGS (39.6) and the Central Administration (46.9). Deflated month by month with the INDEC CPI. Originals: SRC-9009 to SRC-9012.*

In 2025 the interest recorded in the budget was 2.3 times transfers to national universities; between January and September 2026, 2.9 times (13.1 against 4.5 trillion pesos at August 2026 prices). If the interest that peso debt capitalized in 2025, which the budget does not record as interest, is added, the ratio becomes nine to ten times. The comparison serves to measure the relative size of the cut to universities against other flows. It does not say that university spending is efficient, or that it has no fiscal cost, or that a peso of interest can simply be reallocated to another use, because interest payments are contractual obligations.

Two further caveats. Both interest columns are accrued and exclude amortization and gross placements, which are debt movements and not spending. And in 2026 there are appropriations for universities not yet accrued (1.5 trillion current pesos under "universidades sin discriminar", unallocated universities), in a year when the application of the University Funding Law (Ley de Financiamiento Universitario, 27.795) is being disputed in court, according to the press.
