# Summary

> Version of 18 October 2026. Debt stock at 30 June 2026, with later operations up to 16 October. Flows from December 2023 to August 2026. Figures in the summary are rounded; the detail, with its source, is in each chapter. Pesos are reported in pesos and US dollars (USD) in dollars. Every acronym is explained in the glossary at the end.

Argentina's public debt moves in two circuits, one in local currency (pesos) and one in foreign currency (dollars). The peso circuit is run by the Treasury and the Banco Central de la República Argentina (BCRA, the central bank), which issue pesos, place securities in exchange for pesos already issued, and roll those securities over or redeem them, returning the pesos to the holders together with the interest due. The dollar circuit is run by exporters, who bring dollars in when they are paid for goods and services sold abroad, and by lenders, foreign or local holders of saved dollars, who advance them in exchange for repayment at a future date, plus interest. This report measures each circuit in its own currency, shows where they meet, and asks who won and who lost in each during the term that began in December 2023.

Beyond how much the State owes, the report asks which obligations require resources from third parties, in which currency they must be paid, and how that currency is obtained. A fall in debt owed to other parts of the public sector can reduce the published total while commitments to other creditors rise. And a fiscal surplus in pesos can coexist with a persistent need for dollar financing. The report reconstructs those differences and the flows that connect them.

**How much is owed in each currency.** At the end of June 2026 the Treasury owed about 334 trillion pesos payable in pesos and about USD 247 billion payable in dollars or other foreign currencies. Converted to a single currency, the two parts weigh almost the same: 48% in pesos and 52% in foreign currency. Hence the description of a half-and-half debt.

**Who it is owed to.** A large part of the debt is owed by the State to itself.

| | Payable in pesos | Payable in foreign currency |
|---|---:|---:|
| To the public sector itself (BCRA, Fondo de Garantía de Sustentabilidad or FGS, Banco Nación) | 200 trillion pesos | USD 62,000 million |
| To private holders, resident and non-resident | 134 trillion pesos | USD 85,000 million |
| To international organizations (International Monetary Fund or IMF, and others) | | USD 99,000 million |
| **Total** | **334 trillion pesos** | **USD 247,000 million** |

Outside the public sector, then, there remain about 134 trillion pesos and about USD 185 billion. That is the debt the State has to pay to others.

**First result: third-party debt, in each currency.** In dollars, the published total fell and third-party debt rose. Between December 2023 and June 2026 the debt payable in foreign currency fell by about USD 18 billion: the part owed to the public sector itself fell by about 32 billion, mainly through buybacks of Letras Intransferibles (non-transferable Treasury notes held by the BCRA), and the part owed to outside creditors rose by about 14 billion, with 20 billion more owed to the IMF and the other international organizations and 6 billion less owed to private holders. In pesos, the result depends on which debtor one looks at. The Treasury's peso debt to private holders grew 53% in real terms, from about 91 to about 139 trillion pesos at August 2026 prices. But at the end of 2023 the largest peso debt to third parties belonged to the BCRA, in pases (repos absorbing pesos) and Letras de Liquidez (LELIQ), and that debt moved to the Treasury. Counting the Treasury and the BCRA together, interest-bearing peso debt to third parties fell 21%, from about 180 to about 142 trillion pesos at August 2026 prices. The transfer only changed the debtor; the fall came from inflation in the first months and from the replacement of interest-bearing debt by monetary base, which is also a BCRA liability even though it pays no interest.

**Second result: where the dollars came from.** The Treasury recorded foreign-currency debt payments of about USD 100.6 billion. About 16.7 billion of that total was dollar debt to the BCRA that the Treasury redeemed with pesos: securities with a face value of 28 billion, which the BCRA handed over at a discount. That part moved no dollars, because it only changed a debt inside the public sector. The other 83.9 billion was paid with dollars, which came from two places: about 64.2 billion of new foreign-currency debt, mainly from international organizations, and about 26.8 billion bought with pesos, almost all from the BCRA, for about 46 trillion pesos at August 2026 prices. The two sources add up to 91 billion, about 7 billion more than what was paid: 2.7 billion stayed in the Treasury's deposits at the BCRA and 4.3 billion is a difference the source does not break down (chapter 5). In the FX market, exporters brought in about USD 225 billion, and the second-largest outflow, after imports, was dollar purchases by individuals and firms: about 48 billion net, almost all after April 2025, more than twice the increase in reserves. From abroad came about 15 billion in IMF disbursements since April 2025 and 6 billion in bank repos with the BCRA. The agreement with the US Treasury, for up to 20 billion, was drawn for 2.5 billion over two months. The BCRA's net reserves ended almost where they started: about −8.5 billion at the end of 2023 and about −6.4 billion in mid-2026, according to our own estimate.

**Third result: the cost of peso debt and how it is recorded.** Until July 2024 the BCRA paid the interest on the pesos it absorbed, about 35 trillion pesos at August 2026 prices over the term. Since then the Treasury pays it, almost all as interest added to the principal of its bills and bonds: between 5 and 11 trillion current pesos per quarter, about 66 trillion up to June 2026. The cash-basis balance published by the Budget Secretariat (Secretaría de Hacienda) does not record it. With that interest included, the overall balance of the national public sector has been in deficit every quarter since mid-2024, and the measure still leaves out the principal adjustment of bonds linked to the Coeficiente de Estabilización de Referencia (CER, the inflation-indexation coefficient). For scale: in 2025 capitalized interest was 40 to 47 trillion pesos at August 2026 prices, the interest recorded in the budget 14 trillion, and transfers to national universities 6 trillion.

**Between the Treasury and the BCRA.** The dollar debt changed creditor. The Letras Intransferibles the Treasury owes the BCRA fell from USD 67 billion to USD 30 billion, and debt to the IMF rose from 41 billion to 58 billion.

**What lies ahead.** In the twelve months from July 2026 to June 2027 about USD 19 billion falls due to creditors outside the public sector, and between 2028 and 2031, 25 to 27 billion a year if nobody refinances. The IMF projects a goods trade surplus of 21 to 29 billion a year between 2027 and 2031, which stays below those maturities until 2029 and slightly above them in 2030 and 2031. After paying for services, profits and interest, the projected current account remains in deficit every year: the country as a whole does not generate net dollars to repay principal, which has to be refinanced. That balance also belongs to the country and not to the Treasury, which has to buy the dollars or borrow them.

**Opacity.** The debt stock is transparent; the movements are not. The fiscal accounts classify debt by its currency of denomination and not by its currency of payment: reading all of the term's amortization "in foreign currency" as foreign-currency payments would overstate them by 60%. There are no public series for the Treasury's dollar deposits, for its foreign-currency purchases by channel, or for the holdings of each bond, and the figures for a single operation change from one official publication to another.

**Collecting export duties in dollars (Annex A).** The model compares the current regime with the proposal that exporters pay the duty directly in dollars. If the BCRA buys all the dollars in the market, both options leave the public sector with the same dollars and the same pesos; what changes is which pocket holds them. The difference appears when the BCRA does not buy, as happened from April 2025: all else equal, collecting in dollars would have kept in the public sector about USD 12 billion that ended up in private hands. This is a conditional result, without the responses of exporters, buyers or the exchange rate. Looking ahead, the duties are enough to pay about one fifth of foreign-currency maturities owed to third parties, and a stabilization fund halves the month-to-month variation in what the Treasury receives.
