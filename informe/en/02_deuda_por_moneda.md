# 2. Debt by currency and by holder

This chapter answers three questions about the stock: how much debt is payable in each currency, who holds it and when the dollar part matures. The perimeter is the Central Administration, whose debt is recorded in the Sistema de Gestión y Análisis de la Deuda (SIGADE, the debt management and analysis system), the database of the Oficina Nacional de Crédito Público (ONCP, the National Public Credit Office).

A warning about units. The Secretaría de Finanzas (Finance Secretariat) publishes all debt in dollars, including peso debt, converted at the exchange rate of each cut-off date. The report keeps those figures as the source publishes them, because that is the only way to reconcile with it, and adds the peso debt expressed in pesos using the BCRA's reference exchange rate (Comunicación A 3500) for the same date. That conversion is our own and is flagged each time. The SRC codes identify each source in Annex C, and the batches (AJ, AM and others) are the data deliveries described in chapter 8.

::: {.destacado custom-style="Destacado"}
**Total foreign-currency debt fell; foreign-currency debt to third parties rose.**

Between December 2023 and June 2026, debt payable in foreign currency fell by USD 17,945 million, from 264,481 to 246,536 million. The part owed to the public sector itself fell by 32,298 million, mainly because of the buybacks of Letras Intransferibles (non-transferable Treasury notes held by the BCRA) from the BCRA, and the part owed to outside creditors rose by 14,353 million: 20,360 million more with the IMF and the other international organizations, and 6,007 million less with private creditors.

*Scope.* Both dates have the same perimeter: performing Central Administration debt, excluding defaulted debt that has not been restructured. The breakdown of private holdings by currency is our own calculation, using percentages that the Finance Secretariat publishes to one decimal place (margin of about ±175 million).

Three details of this table matter for the rest of the report.

- **Dólar linked debt is denominated in dollars and payable in pesos.** The report counts it in the peso circuit. The Finance Secretariat bulletin does the same in table A.3, but not in A.1, which classifies it by currency of account. The same bond changes sides depending on which sheet one looks at (chapter 7).
- **The Letras Intransferibles are Treasury dollar debt owed to the BCRA.** When the public sector is consolidated, they cancel out against the BCRA's asset. Their amount fell from USD 67,190 million in December 2023 to 30,310 million in June 2026, because of the buybacks described in chapter 5.
- **The FODER guarantee notes are not firm debt.** The Treasury delivers them as collateral and pays only if a renewable energy generator exercises a put option. Otherwise they expire unpaid every 31 December. SIGADE records them as ordinary debt; the report treats them as contingent.

**A caution about the private column.** The private figures in this table add up all currencies: they include peso, CER and dólar linked debt, converted to dollars, and defaulted debt not restructured. Between December 2023 and June 2026 they grew by about USD 52 billion, and almost all of that increase is peso debt. The part payable to them in foreign currency fell by 6,007 million (section 2.2). Section 2.4 breaks down each currency in its own unit.

Foreign-currency debt to creditors outside the public sector rose by about USD 14.4 billion during the term, and almost all of that increase is with the IMF. In the same 33 months, individuals and companies made net purchases of USD 48,476 million for hoarding (chapter 4), and the BCRA's net reserves, measured as the IMF program measures them, went from about −8.5 billion at end-2023 (IMF, SRC-1271) to about −6.4 billion at end-June 2026 (own estimate, chapter 4). The public sector borrowed dollars from the IMF while the private sector took out of the FX market almost three times the increase in IMF debt, and net reserves ended up almost where they started.

Four out of every ten dollars of gross debt are debt the state owes itself: Letras Intransferibles at the BCRA, bonds in the FGS of the Administración Nacional de la Seguridad Social (ANSES, the social security administration), notes at Banco Nación and in trust funds. Of what the public sector holds, 31.5% is in dollars and the rest in pesos (40.0% CER, 27.4% nominal pesos, 1.1% dólar linked).

In peso debt the share is larger: the public sector holds 60% (USD 135,084 million, about 200 trillion pesos), and 70% of CER debt. The report identified the holder for 93% of that portion: the BCRA holds 52,843 million, the FGS 52,644 million and Banco Nación 19,828 million. Outside the public sector there remain about USD 90 billion, about 134 trillion pesos, in banks, mutual funds, insurers and individuals. The Finance Secretariat does not publish how much each of them holds (chapter 7).

In dollar debt, the public sector directly holds the Letras Intransferibles (30,310), the dollar note held by the public sector (1,228) and the FODER notes (3,774). It also holds Globales and Bonares, which the report estimates at USD 6,739 million using the BCRA's financial statements. For the rest, about 20 billion that cannot be identified, the report allocates public-sector holdings in proportion to those published by the Finance Secretariat, and flags this as an assumption.

## 2.4 Third-party debt in each currency

The previous section measures third-party debt in dollars, because that is how the Finance Secretariat publishes it. This one breaks it down by currency. For the peso part, the report converts the Finance Secretariat's dollars back into pesos at the same exchange rate used to convert them (A 3500 for each cut-off date), and brings them to August 2026 prices with the CPI. That way the change one sees is the change in the debt, not in the exchange rate.

On the peso side, the BCRA also has to be considered. At end-2023 the cost of absorbing pesos sat in its own interest-bearing liabilities, the pases and LELIQ, which are also public-sector debt owed to banks. Chapter 3 explains how that debt moved to the Treasury. Looking at the Treasury alone, the transfer shows up as new debt.

Treasury peso debt to private creditors grew fivefold in current pesos and by 53% in constant pesos. But the BCRA's interest-bearing liabilities almost disappeared, and with them the public sector's peso debt to third parties, counting the Treasury and the BCRA together, fell by 21% in constant pesos. Behind that fall are three movements that the report cannot separate precisely: the transfer of absorption from the BCRA to the Treasury, which changed the debtor without changing the debt; the inflation of December 2023 and the first months of 2024, which, with interest rates below inflation, reduced the real value of the stock; and the increase in the monetary base, from 33.4 to 47.5 trillion pesos at August 2026 prices, which is money and pays no interest.

The result for the term therefore depends on the perimeter. With the Treasury alone, obligations to third parties rose in both currencies. With the Treasury and the BCRA together, they rose in foreign currency (USD 14,353 million) and fell in pesos (38 trillion pesos at August 2026 prices). The underlying difference between the two still stands: peso debt can be rolled over or paid by the state itself, within the limits set out in chapter 1, while foreign-currency debt requires obtaining dollars.

Between 2028 and 2034 the gross need forms a plateau of 27 to 35 billion per year. The 2032 to 2034 peak is explained mainly by the Letras Intransferibles maturing in those years (29,080 million in three years), which are payments to the BCRA and in practice are rolled over. Without them, the need vis-à-vis creditors outside the public sector peaks in 2031 and 2032, at between 27 and 30 billion per year, and starts to decline in 2033. The IMF accounts for between 30% and 37% of the 2028 to 2031 plateau, and the bonds from the 2020 exchange for between 11.7 and 14.9 billion per year until 2035. From 2036 on, the need drops below 7 billion per year.

**Third parties and the trade surplus.** If, in addition to the notes, public-sector holdings of Globales and Bonares are deducted (section 2.3), what must be paid to outside creditors is 21,982 million in 2027, 25,740 million in 2028, 26,938 million in 2029, 24,703 million in 2030 and 26,107 million in 2031. Those dollars have to be compared with what the country can earn by selling more than it buys. The IMF, in the second review of the agreement (SRC-1263, table 2), projects a goods trade surplus of 21.4 billion in 2027, 23.2 billion in 2028, 25.4 billion in 2029, 27.2 billion in 2030 and 28.6 billion in 2031.

Until 2029 maturities exceed the projected goods surplus; in 2030 and 2031 the surplus exceeds them by about 2.5 billion. The goods surplus does not net out any interest, so it can be compared with full debt service, principal plus interest. Even so, the comparison is optimistic, for three reasons.

The first is that the country also pays for services, profits and interest. The same IMF projection shows a current account deficit every year, from 4.5 billion in 2027 to 2.2 billion in 2031. The current account already nets out interest on public debt, so full debt service cannot be subtracted from it without counting that interest twice. What the current account shows is that, even after paying interest, the country as a whole does not generate net dollars, and all maturing principal has to be refinanced or covered with financial account inflows.

The second is that this balance belongs to the country and not to the Treasury. The country's external constraint and the Treasury's foreign-currency cash position are different things: the Treasury has to buy those dollars with pesos, from the BCRA or in the market, or borrow them. The third is that the projection changes a lot from one report to the next: for 2027 the IMF projected 22.3 billion in April 2025, 15 billion in August 2025 and 21.4 billion in May 2026. Without refinancing, the dollar savings the country could generate are not enough to pay the dollar debt that falls due; with refinancing by the international organizations, which is what happened during the term (chapter 4), the pressure falls to about 12 to 16 billion per year.

## 2.6 What reconciles and what does not

All reconciliation checks pass: the SIGADE balance against the official spreadsheet, the four flow identities for the second quarter of 2026 and the official twelve-month maturity profile in its 24 cells (twelve months for principal and for interest).

The engine's calendar shows USD 32,913 million more principal over twelve months than the official profile. The difference is explained with no residual. There are 36,328 million of operations after the cut-off date, 3,638 million of capitalized interest that SIGADE does not project and 943 million of index lags, against 7,997 million of flows that have no amount until the rates are known. Almost all of the difference is in pesos. The need for foreign currency does not change.

After the cut-off date, 67 operations settled up to 16 October 2026 were loaded: issues, reopenings, exchanges and redemptions, almost all in pesos. Provisional gross debt for August 2026 is USD 484,917 million. Still unexplained are a payment of 1,040 million under Resolución Conjunta (RC, joint resolution) 30/2026 that does not appear in the Treasury's deposits, the balance reductions of three bills (TMF27, TMG27 and TZX27) without an operation to justify them, and a 318 million reduction in the FODER notes in June 2026.
