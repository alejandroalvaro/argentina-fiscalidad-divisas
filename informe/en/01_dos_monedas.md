# 1. Two currencies, two circuits

::: {.destacado custom-style="Destacado"}
**Expressing peso debt in dollars does not turn it into an obligation to obtain dollars.**

The Argentine state can issue pesos. To pay dollars, it has to obtain them. Of the USD 471,690 million of performing debt at 30 June 2026, USD 225,154 million (48%) is payable in pesos, including USD 9,595 million that is denominated in dollars and payable in pesos.

*Scope.* The currency of payment defines the contractual need for foreign currency. Dollar-indexed debt requires no foreign currency, but its peso amount rises with the exchange rate, and that is an indirect fiscal and exchange-rate risk.
:::

## 1.1 The question

Argentina's public debt is usually presented as a single number. At the end of June 2026 that number was about USD 472 billion, and it anchors the discussion of the debt-to-GDP ratio, debt sustainability and the credibility of the economic program. The number helps with some questions, but it hides the one that matters most in an economy like Argentina's: in which currency the debt must be paid and where that currency comes from.

This report starts from a simple observation. The Argentine state handles two currencies that it cannot convert into each other by its own decision.

Pesos are issued by the Banco Central de la República Argentina (BCRA, the central bank), and the Treasury borrows, spends and collects them. That is the logical order: for someone to pay a tax in pesos or lend pesos to the Treasury, those pesos must have been created first, by the BCRA or by the banks, and ultimately the system rests on the currency the state issues. The chronological sequence is a different matter, because in institutional practice the three things happen at the same time. The Treasury holds deposits, places debt, collects taxes and pays expenses every day, and in a given fiscal year it may collect before it spends or spend before it places debt. The logical order implies that the state does not need to collect pesos in order to spend them, even though its rules require it to finance spending with tax revenue or with debt.

Dollars, by contrast, are issued by no one in the country. Central bank money in dollars, meaning notes and bank reserves at the Federal Reserve, is issued only by the US Federal Reserve; dollar deposits are created by banks when they lend, in the United States and outside it, and rest on that money, just as peso deposits rest on the BCRA's. Neither the BCRA nor the Treasury can create either form. They enter Argentina when someone sells something abroad or when someone lends, and they leave when the country imports, pays debt or someone decides to keep them.

A fiscal surplus in pesos means that the state took more pesos from the private sector through taxes than it put in through spending and interest payments. The pesos that return to the state through taxes stop circulating, because the state does not have to give them back to anyone: collecting taxes is the way to withdraw pesos from the economy, just as spending is the way to put them in. The surplus is therefore a quantity of pesos the state withdrew, and it is measured in pesos.

To pay a dollar debt with those pesos, someone has to sell the dollars and the Treasury has to buy them. Pesos do not turn into dollars: they change hands and continue to exist. Before, they were in the Treasury's account; after the purchase, they are in the hands of whoever sold the dollars, who can spend them, lend them or use them to buy other dollars.

Hence the idea that organizes the report: public debt moves in two circuits.

- **The peso circuit** is run by the Treasury and the BCRA. They can issue money, place debt, refinance it and set or influence the rate it pays. Its limits depend on how fast the economy and credit grow, which is what drives the demand for pesos, on the willingness of banks and funds to roll over what matures and, in Argentina's case, on the willingness of savers to keep their savings in pesos or swap them for dollars.
- **The dollar circuit** is run by others: exporters, who bring in the foreign currency and decide when to sell it in the FX market, and lenders (the International Monetary Fund, or IMF, the other international organizations and bondholders), who advance dollars and are repaid in dollars. The public sector can regulate that circuit, but it cannot create its currency.

The two circuits touch at few points: when the Treasury buys dollars with pesos, when the BCRA buys or sells foreign currency and, during this term, when the Treasury pays off with pesos debt it owed the BCRA in dollars. Another case looks like a crossing and is not one: debt denominated in dollars and payable in pesos, such as dólar linked bonds. No peso is exchanged for any dollar there. The dollar works as an adjustment index for the amount due, just as inflation does in a CER bond, and the debt is paid entirely in pesos. Presenting it as dollar debt, as the fiscal statistics do, suggests a need for foreign currency that does not exist and makes both circuits more opaque.

The report measures each circuit in its own currency, shows where they cross and how much passed through each crossing between December 2023 and August 2026, and asks who ended up with the pesos and who with the dollars.

Beyond how much the state owes, the report asks which obligations require resources vis-à-vis third parties, in which currency they must be paid and how that currency is obtained. A fall in debt owed to entities within the public sector itself can reduce the published total while commitments to other creditors increase. A fiscal surplus in pesos can coexist with a persistent need for dollar financing. And public-sector financing, exchange-rate policy and private purchases of dollars are better explained as a joint process than separately. The report reconstructs those differences and the flows that connect them.

## 1.2 Why measure each currency separately

Adding pesos and dollars at an exchange rate is useful for some comparisons, such as debt to GDP or across countries, but it does not describe which currency each part must be paid in. Three reasons justify keeping them apart.

**Because they are not substitutes.** A peso of surplus does not pay a dollar of maturities without going through the FX market or the reserves. The conversion is a transaction with a counterparty, a price and consequences for the monetary base, so it is better shown as a transaction than as an exchange rate applied in a spreadsheet.

**Because the exchange rate moves the totals without anything happening.** Between December 2023 and August 2026 the official exchange rate and prices moved at very different paces depending on the stretch. A stock of peso debt expressed in dollars rises or falls with the exchange rate even if nobody has placed or paid anything. Measured in pesos, and in constant pesos when years are compared, the movement one sees is that of the debt.

**Because the same words mean different things in each currency.** "Foreign-currency" debt may be denominated in dollars and payable in pesos (dólar linked bonds, the Bono del Tesoro Nacional (BONTE, a Treasury bond) and the dual bonds that pay in pesos), and peso debt may not require a single dollar over its whole life. The report classifies each flow by currency of payment, which determines which circuit the money comes from, and keeps the currency of denomination, the indexation and the holder as separate fields.

For that reason, in this report pesos are reported in pesos and dollars in dollars. When a peso flow is converted for comparison, the exchange rate is stated, along with a note that the conversion does not make those dollars available. Pesos from different months are presented at August 2026 prices, deflated by the consumer price index, unless stated otherwise. Each chart shows a single currency.

## 1.3 The case and the period

The period runs from December 2023, when the current government took office, to August 2026, the last month with complete data in all the sources used. It is a period of large changes in both circuits:

- on the peso side, the BCRA stopped paying interest on its own liabilities and sterilization moved to the Treasury, first with the Letras Fiscales de Liquidez (LEFI, Treasury liquidity bills) and then with Letras Capitalizables (LECAP, capitalizing Treasury bills), Bonos Capitalizables (BONCAP, capitalizing Treasury bonds) and dual bonds; the Treasury showed a surplus on a cash basis while it capitalized interest that does not appear in that balance;
- on the dollar side, a new agreement with the IMF was signed in April 2025, restrictions on the purchase of foreign currency by individuals were lifted that same month, private dollar hoarding again became the largest outflow from the FX market and the Treasury began buying foreign currency with pesos to pay its debt.

The debt stock is measured at 30 June 2026, the latest published cut of the Sistema de Gestión y Análisis de la Deuda (SIGADE, the debt management and analysis system), with later operations loaded from the resolutions and auctions. Flows are measured month by month from December 2023.

The argument is not specific to Argentina. It holds, with nuances, for any economy that trades with the world in a currency it does not issue. Brazil serves as a limited contrast (Annex B): a large economy in the region with public debt almost entirely in its own currency and an exchange-rate regime that lets exporters decide where to hold their foreign currency.

## 1.4 What the report contributes

Neither the distinction between peso and dollar debt nor the recording of capitalized interest is a new idea: analysts, consultancies and the Jefatura de Gabinete (Cabinet Office) itself discuss them. The contribution lies in reconstructing them in an integrated way, quantifying them for the whole term and making each figure traceable to its original document.

1. **The two circuits measured separately and closed.** Four flow diagrams for the term, each in a single currency, that close against their sources: the FX market and the Treasury in dollars, the national public sector and the BCRA in pesos.
2. **The currency crossing quantified.** How many pesos the Treasury applied to dollar obligations, through which channel (purchase of foreign currency or repayment with pesos of dollar debt owed to the BCRA) and what remains unexplained, setting apart the debt denominated in dollars and payable in pesos, which requires no foreign currency.
3. **A calendar of foreign-currency payments built contract by contract**, consolidated by holder, that separates what must be paid to third parties from what the state owes itself.
4. **Capitalized interest, quarter by quarter.** A series of capitalized interest on peso debt, net of what placements bring in, to compare the published cash-basis balance with one that includes that interest.
5. **Opacity as a finding.** What had to be gathered to reconstruct the two circuits, which series do not exist and which figures change between official publications.
6. **A reproducible method.** Every figure can be recomputed from the original documents, stored and identified by their SHA-256 hash, with the repository's code.

## 1.5 How the report is organized

Chapter 2 presents the debt by currency and by holder. Chapters 3 and 4 go through the peso circuit and the dollar circuit, and chapter 5 measures where they cross. Chapter 6 asks who wins and who loses in each circuit. Chapter 7 documents the opacity, and chapter 8 describes the method. Annex A applies the framework to a specific proposal, collecting export duties in dollars (Mosler, 2022), and presents the results of the model that compares the regimes. Annex B presents the contrast with Brazil. Each acronym is spelled out the first time it appears, and the glossary at the end explains each acronym, instrument and concept; in the web version, the first appearance of each glossary term links to its definition.
