# 5. Where they cross

The two circuits meet at the Treasury. The Treasury owes dollars, and most of its revenue is in pesos. This chapter measures how many dollars the Treasury needed during the term that began in December 2023, where it got them and how many pesos it spent to obtain them. Dollars are reported in USD million and pesos in trillion pesos at August 2026 prices.

::: {.destacado custom-style="Destacado"}
**A peso surplus does not bring in dollars by itself.**

The Treasury recorded USD 100,639 million in debt payments in foreign currency. Of that total, 16,703 was debt to the BCRA canceled with pesos (a face value of 28,030, delivered at a discount), and 83,936 was paid with dollars. Those dollars came from new foreign-currency debt (64,150) and from purchases with pesos (26,810); the surplus went to deposits at the BCRA (2,715) or remains as a difference the source does not break down (4,309).

*Scope.* The three channels have different economic content. Buying dollars in the market takes them out of private hands; buying them from the BCRA takes them out of reserves; canceling a dollar debt to the BCRA with pesos obtains no usable dollar, because it only changes a debt within the public sector.
:::

In almost three years the Treasury recorded USD 100,639 million of principal and interest on foreign-currency debt (76,080 plus 24,559). The account reads better if one part is set apart. Of those payments, 16,703 was the cancellation with pesos of dollar debt to the BCRA: the financing account records it as foreign-currency amortization, and that is why it also appears among the inflows, even though no dollar came in or went out. The remaining 83,936 was paid with dollars. With new foreign-currency debt, mostly from international organizations, the Treasury obtained 64,150, and it bought 26,810 with pesos. Those two sources add up to 90,960, 7,024 more than what was paid: 2,715 stayed in the Treasury's deposits at the BCRA and 4,309 is the difference the source does not break down. The two channels that go through pesos are best read separately, because they are not equivalent.

**It bought dollars with pesos.** The total was USD 26,810 million: 25,281 directly from the BCRA and 1,529 net in the market. They cost ARS 45.7 trillion at August 2026 prices. Purchases were concentrated in 2024 (15,641 million from the BCRA, with months of 3,822 in July and 2,814 in November), fell in 2025 (2,715 from the BCRA and 1,258 net in the market) and returned in 2026 (6,925 from the BCRA and 271 in the market through August). In October 2025, with the exchange rate at the ceiling of the band, the Treasury sold 2,060 million in the market.

The figures by channel come from the BCRA monthly report, which is the series the diagram uses, with one correction. On 5 February 2026 the Treasury bought 787 million from the BCRA to pay the IMF: the BCRA daily series (variable 48) and the press record it, and the monthly report does not count it as a direct purchase. Without it, the Treasury's February dollar account was short by 731 million; with it, the month's difference falls to 56. The daily series gives 25,234 million from the BCRA for the whole period, 47 less than the corrected figure: these are differences of less than 60 million per month between the two series, which the report leaves as they are (chapter 7).

**It canceled with pesos dollar debt it owed the BCRA.** There were two operations, for a face value of USD 28,030 million. The Treasury paid ARS 27.0 trillion at August 2026 prices for them, equivalent to USD 16,703 million at each month's average exchange rate. That is the figure the financing account records as amortization, and that is why it enters the table; the difference from face value, about USD 11.3 billion, is the discount at which the BCRA delivered the securities:

- in February 2024 the Treasury bought AL35 bonds from the BCRA for a face value of USD 7,596 million and paid ARS 2.951 trillion in pesos of that time, about USD 3,534 million, less than half of face value (RC 13/2024, SRC-2064);
- in May 2026 it bought back Letras Intransferibles (non-transferable Treasury notes held by the BCRA) for a face value of 20,434 million and paid ARS 18.4 trillion, about USD 13,225 million at the exchange rate of the operation (ARS 1,391.31) and 13,169 at the monthly average (RC 27/2026, SRC-2031 and SRC-2047). The pesos came from the BCRA's profits for fiscal year 2025, which totaled ARS 24.4 trillion: 18.4 went to the buyback and 6 to deposits.

*USD million except pesos.*

The difference the source does not break down, 4,309 million, is what could not be attributed to any of those channels. It equals 4.0% of the Treasury's dollar flow, and no month exceeds 1,600 million in absolute value. The largest months (December 2025, −1,586; June 2025, +1,455; May 2024, +1,371, and April 2026, −1,357) have opposite signs and coincide with operations that one source records in one month and the other in another. The report treats it as a timing and recording mismatch and leaves it visible, without allocating it. Adding the February 2026 purchase raised the total difference from 3,522 to 4,309 million, even though February itself ended up close to zero: the sum of the months offsets errors of opposite sign, so a smaller total does not guarantee a better fit.

## 5.2 What looks like a crossing and is not

A large part of the debt that the statistics call "foreign-currency" never went through the dollar circuit. These are bonds denominated in dollars that are paid in pesos: dólar linked bonds, the BONTE, the duales that pay in pesos and the dollar-linked bills.

- The Treasury paid in pesos USD 45,325 million of that debt (49,487 in cash, less the 4,162 million of the BONAR DUAL TDE25 that the BCRA converted into LECAP a week before maturity, without cash, under RC 5/2025). In pesos this was ARS 79.3 trillion at August 2026 prices.
- In the same period it placed 19,413 million of debt denominated in dollars and collected in pesos, ARS 31.9 trillion.

Those flows belong to the peso circuit (chapter 3), because the pesos come in and go out as pesos. Statistics that classify by currency of issue count them as foreign-currency debt, and chapter 7 shows how that mix makes it harder to read the actual need for dollars.

## 5.3 The crossing seen from the peso side

On the peso side, the crossing adds up to ARS 72.7 trillion at August 2026 prices: ARS 45.7 trillion in dollar purchases and ARS 27.0 trillion in debt to the BCRA canceled with pesos. These are two different uses of pesos. With the first, the Treasury obtained dollars it could use to pay third parties; with the second, it canceled a debt to the BCRA without obtaining any usable dollar. The difference between currencies that the source does not break down appears here as an inflow of ARS 7.7 trillion, the counterpart of the 4,309 million on the dollar side.

For scale, the Treasury applied more pesos to dollar obligations than it collected in export duties over the whole period (ARS 28.6 trillion), and almost as much as it received in BCRA profits (ARS 42.9 trillion). These are comparisons of magnitude within the same currency. The fact that the Treasury has pesos does not mean there are dollars to buy: that is the starting point of the report.

## 5.4 The Letras Intransferibles: a crossing within the public sector

Between 2025 and 2026 the Treasury bought back Letras Intransferibles from the BCRA for a face value close to USD 38.6 billion, and paid about USD 28 billion for them. There were three channels:

|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| RC 21/2025, May 2025 | 13,617 | 12,000 | dollars from the IMF disbursement | SRC-1257 |
| RC 37/2025, August 2025 | 3,269 | 2,000 | dollars from the IMF disbursement | SRC-2042 |
| RC 27/2026, May 2026 | 20,434 | 13,225 (ARS 18.4 trillion) | pesos from BCRA profits | SRC-2031, SRC-2047 |
| RC 47/2026, August 2026 | 1,303 | 781.5 | dollars the BCRA paid when it bought BOPREAL from the Treasury | SRC-2043 (press, medium confidence) |

*USD million.*

Taken alone, a buyback between the Treasury and the BCRA does not change the public sector's debt to third parties: it changes the composition of the two balance sheets. But the first two were paid with the IMF disbursement, and taken together with that disbursement they do change it: the public sector canceled 16,886 million of face value of debt to itself and took on 14,000 million of new debt to the IMF, which is an external creditor. In each operation, the BCRA gives up a dollar asset against the Treasury, which it could not sell, and receives liquid dollars or pesos. The Treasury replaces a debt to the BCRA with a debt to the IMF (in the first two) or with the use of the BCRA's own profits (in the third). In the third, moreover, the BCRA ended up being paid with pesos it had created itself. Chapter 6 returns to who wins and who loses in these operations.

## 5.5 What lies ahead

The contractual calendar (chapter 2) gives a consolidated need of USD 18,775 million between July 2026 and June 2027, and a plateau of between USD 27 billion and USD 30 billion per year to creditors outside the public sector between 2028 and 2032. With Treasury dollar deposits of USD 1,230 million on 14 October 2026 and net reserves that the IMF projects at −1.8 billion for year-end, that need is covered through the same three channels used during the term: new foreign-currency debt, dollar purchases with pesos and operations with the BCRA. The official financing program foresees purchases from the BCRA of 6.7 billion in 2026 and 4.9 billion in 2027 (Bloomberg Línea, SRC-1344).

Which of the three channels is used decides who finances the gap. With new debt, the lenders. With market purchases, those who sell dollars, in exchange for pesos that someone has to absorb. With operations with the BCRA, the reserves.
