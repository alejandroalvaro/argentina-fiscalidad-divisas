# 9. Conclusions

The conclusions answer three questions: whether the public sector's financial exposure to third parties fell, how it changed in each currency and what dependence on foreign currency remains.

**Did exposure to third parties fall? In dollars, no; in pesos, it depends on the perimeter.** Debt payable in foreign currency fell by USD 17,945 million between December 2023 and June 2026, but all of that decline was debt of the public sector to itself: the BCRA's Letras Intransferibles (non-transferable Treasury notes) fell by 36,880 million. To outside creditors, foreign-currency debt rose by 14,353 million, because of the IMF and the other international organizations. In pesos, the Treasury's debt to private holders grew 53% in real terms, and the BCRA's debt, in pases (repos absorbing pesos) and LELIQ, almost disappeared. Counting both, interest-bearing peso debt to third parties fell 21% in constant pesos. The transfer of absorption from the BCRA to the Treasury changed the debtor without changing the obligation, so it does not explain that fall. What reduced the balance was inflation in the first months, which shrank the real value of the stock, and the replacement of interest-bearing debt by monetary base. The base is a BCRA liability that pays no interest: adding it, the peso liabilities of the two to third parties fell 11%.

**How were the dollars paid?** The Treasury recorded USD 100,639 million of principal and interest payments in foreign currency. Of that, 16,703 was dollar debt to the BCRA canceled with pesos (face value 28,030), an operation that moved no dollars. It paid the other 83,936 with 64,150 of new foreign-currency debt and 26,810 bought with pesos, almost all from the BCRA, for ARS 45.7 trillion at August 2026 prices; the remainder went to deposits (2,715) or is a difference the source does not break down (4,309). The primary surplus for the period, ARS 46.1 trillion, was of a similar magnitude to the cost of the dollar purchases; the available sources do not allow those purchases to be attributed to a specific source of pesos. Whether there were dollars to buy depended on someone selling them: the BCRA, with reserves sustained by the IMF and the repos.

**What role did private dollarization and external financing play?** Since April 2025 individuals and firms bought USD 68,452 million in the official market, and in net terms the term closed with 48,476 million of purchases, more than double the increase in reserves. Over the same period the IMF disbursed about USD 15 billion, banks lent 6 billion in repos and the US Treasury opened a 20 billion line that was used for 2.5 billion for two months. Private agents acquired dollar assets while the public sector took on dollar liabilities to third parties. The report records the coincidence; the causal relationship between the two movements, and the claim that without external support the program would have collapsed, are interpretations that the data in this work do not demonstrate.

**How much did peso debt cost and where is it recorded?** The cost of absorbing pesos changed payer. Until July 2024 the BCRA paid it, ARS 34.6 trillion at August 2026 prices, and it was collected mostly by banks. Since then the Treasury pays it, mostly as capitalized interest: between 5 and 11 trillion current pesos per quarter, which the cash-basis balance does not record. With that interest, the overall balance of the national public sector has been in deficit every quarter since the third quarter of 2024, still without counting the CER principal adjustment.

**What dependence on foreign currency remains?** Between July 2026 and June 2027, USD 18,775 million falls due to third parties, and between 2028 and 2031, from 24,700 to 26,900 million per year, without assuming refinancing. The goods trade surplus projected by the IMF stays below that figure until 2029. That comparison leaves out the other uses of dollars. With services and private income, between 5,000 and 8,200 million a year are left for debt, and 16,500 to 19,400 million are missing before any purchase of dollars to keep. With purchases by persons and, if their restriction is lifted, by firms, at the observed pace, the yearly gap ranges from about 34,000 to about 61,000 million. The IMF projection closes it with foreign investment growing by more than half over 2025, government debt placements every year and residents' purchases of dollars far below those of 2026. The stock of retained profits and of firms' pesos that would seek to leave when the controls end has no public figure and stays out of the account. In mid-October 2026 the Treasury had USD 1,230 million on deposit.

**Opacity is part of the result.** The debt stock is transparent. The movements are not: the fiscal table classifies by currency of denomination and does not publish the currency of payment next to it; there are no series of Treasury dollar deposits, of FX purchases by channel or of holdings of each bond; and the figures for a single operation change between official publications. Reconstructing the two circuits required gathering more than 1,300 sources and more than 1,200 original documents.

**What it contributes and what it does not.** The distinction between pesos and dollars and capitalized interest were already known. What this report adds is the integrated reconstruction of the two circuits, their quantification for the whole term and the ability to rebuild every figure from its source. The report does not assess whether the strategy of the term was good or bad. Annex A applies the framework to a specific proposal, collecting export duties in dollars: as long as the BCRA buys everything that is sold in the FX market, collecting in dollars only changes the pocket in which the dollars sit; when it does not buy, as since April 2025, collection in dollars keeps in the public sector dollars that would otherwise go to private hands, about USD 11.9 billion over the term if everything else had been the same.

## What is missing for the final version

1. Add the behavioral layer to the model: when exporters sell their FX and pay according to the expected rate, what private agents do when they stop buying the duty dollars, and the effect on the exchange rate (Annex A).
2. Explain the gap between estimated and collected duties since 2023.
3. Incorporate SIGADE and the Finance Secretariat workbook as of 30 September 2026, the September FX balance and the August Informe sobre Bancos, when they are published, and the official June net reserves figure when the IMF approves the third review.
4. Review the CER bond column of table A.1.7 and close the unexplained operations (RC 30/2026, TMF27, TMG27, TZX27).
5. Add the CER principal adjustment to the accrued balance (chapter 3.4) and calculate an inflation-adjusted balance with a uniform criterion for all peso debt.
6. Break down by currency the debt to private holders in December 2024 and 2025, to have the complete series of third-party debt (chapter 2.4).
7. Take from the tables of the IMF reports the levels of the reserve targets, original and revised, and find the amount of the US Treasury's peso purchases in October 2025 (chapter 4.4).
8. Store with a hash the two BCRA press releases on repos that the portal did not allow to be downloaded, and verify in the Boletín Oficial the status of the Ley de Financiamiento Universitario (university funding law) (chapter 3.5).
9. Estimate the stock of retained profits from financial years before 2025 and of pesos held by firms and foreign investors that would seek to leave if the restriction on firms were lifted, and separate from persons' purchases what returns as dollar deposits (chapter 2.6).
